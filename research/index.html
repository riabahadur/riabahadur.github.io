Research

Wargaming, research security, technological change, and scientific governance.

Ria Bahadur's research examines how institutions construct credible judgments under uncertainty—and how technologies, expertise, rules, and organizational structures shape the decisions that follow.

Current research

How does a future become credible enough for an institution to act on it?

Current work centers on military wargaming and research security. Although the domains differ, both involve institutions making consequential judgments with incomplete information: what threat is plausible, which technology matters, whose expertise is authoritative, and when uncertainty justifies action.

The research approaches those judgments as products of design and institutional structure rather than as neutral observations. Wargames encode assumptions through scenarios, time, capability representation, adjudication, and participant roles. Scientific institutions similarly distribute authority through rules governing openness, security, collaboration, expertise, and technological priority.

STS Honors Thesis

Credibility by Design

Credibility by Design: How Military Wargames Shape Plausibility Judgments examines how professional military wargames structure what participants regard as credible futures of conflict.

The project studies scenario framing, temporal pacing, capability sequencing, technological mediation, adjudication authority, and participant expertise—and how judgments produced during gameplay can travel into doctrine, procurement, and strategic planning.

METHOD

Mixed-methods comparative design

Archival-historical analysis, comparative case study, controlled design variation, structured observation, and pre- and post-exercise interviews.

COMPARISON

Gameplay, technology & expertise

Comparison across U.S., NATO-associated, AI-augmented, and traditional tabletop formats, with attention to differences among military planners, technical experts, designers, facilitators, and institutional consumers.

OUTCOME

Plausibility judgments

Analysis of how participants construct, defend, and revise claims about credible conflict futures and how those claims gain institutional authority beyond the exercise.

Research security

How should open scientific systems operate under strategic competition?

Research-security work examines the tension between two institutional requirements that cannot simply be optimized independently: scientific systems depend on exchange, mobility, international collaboration, and trust, while states also seek to protect strategically consequential knowledge and technological capabilities.

Current work at the Hoover Institution focuses on the future of U.S. scientific innovation, technological leadership in AI, research security, international cooperation, scientific prioritization, governance, and public trust.

Research questions

Three questions recur across the current program.

What makes an uncertain future credible?

How do models, game structures, expertise, and technologies determine which futures participants and institutions treat as plausible?

What survives translation between institutions?

How do models, policies, technologies, and analytical frameworks change when they move into environments with different rules, actors, incentives, and forms of authority?

How should scientific openness and security coexist?

Where does collaboration remain indispensable, what genuinely requires protection, and how should scientific institutions govern the boundary between the two?
