Conference Paper · 2026

Ignore the Fool

AI, Scientific Influence, and the Crisis of Validation in Publications and Platforms.

The argument

The information crisis is also a crisis of visible validation.

The paper argues that contemporary information problems cannot be reduced to the existence of more misinformation. AI systems and platform-mediated communication increasingly compress distinctions that once helped audiences interpret scientific claims: peer-reviewed findings, preprints, corrected studies, advertisements, expert interpretation, patient experience, and fabricated references can all arrive in a similarly authoritative register.

The resulting problem is institutional as much as informational. A claim can appear credible after its evidentiary status, provenance, incentives, and transformation history have become difficult to see.

The paper develops metacognitive validation literacy: the ability to reconstruct how a claim was produced, tested, classified, circulated, monetized, transformed, and converted into action.

VALID

A shared structure for interrogating scientific and technical claims.

PROVENANCE

Where did the claim come from?

People, institutions, datasets, publications, systems, and the chain through which the claim originated.

EVIDENCE

What supports it?

Methods, data, assumptions, inferential steps, and the evidence actually available for scrutiny.

VALIDATION

What has been checked?

Review, replication, corroboration, verification, correction, and the limits of what has been validated.

INCENTIVES

What pressures surround it?

Institutional, commercial, reputational, political, and platform-level incentives shaping production and circulation.

TRANSFORMATION

What changed as it travelled?

Summarization, ranking, AI mediation, repetition, translation, platform distribution, and loss of context.

STS Forum work
Why validation

Authority can travel farther than the procedures that produced it.

Scientific institutions historically supplied imperfect but meaningful distinctions among kinds of knowledge. Peer review, professional norms, regulatory procedures, disciplinary methods, and correction mechanisms made different claims carry different evidentiary statuses.

AI and platform systems can detach the language of authority from those distinctions. The paper asks what forms of literacy and institutional design are needed when the audience must increasingly reconstruct those distinctions after the claim has already reached them.
