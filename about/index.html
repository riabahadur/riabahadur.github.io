About

I am interested in what happens when institutions have to act before certainty arrives.

My work moves between science, security, law, education, and technology, but the underlying question is remarkably consistent: how do people and institutions make consequential decisions when the future is uncertain, expertise is incomplete, and getting it wrong has real human costs?

The thread

I do not think of these as separate careers.

I am a researcher, legislative author, and founder whose work has developed across several seemingly different systems: sex-trafficking prevention, military wargaming, research security, science governance, education, and emerging technology.

What connects them is not a particular institution or policy area. It is an interest in how ideas survive contact with reality.

A model can look coherent until participants enter the game. A policy intervention can work in one state and fail in another. Scientific openness can appear uncomplicated until security, geopolitical competition, and institutional trust collide. An educational resource can be well-intentioned and still fail the people it is meant to serve if its assumptions about them are wrong.

I am drawn to those moments: where the model meets the institution, where the institution meets the person, and where someone has to decide what to do next.

Where it began

Prevention before policy.

I began working on sex-trafficking prevention when I was twelve. The work started with education rather than legislation: trying to understand why common narratives about sexual violence and trafficking often failed to describe the people actually affected by them.

That eventually became educational material for students and educators, followed by a professional guide intended for Sexual Assault Nurse Examiners.

Only later did the question become legislative: if prevention education matters, how can a public system make it durable?

Law as an experiment in institutions

Passing one law taught me what law could do. Trying to translate it taught me what law could not do automatically.

At seventeen, I created, drafted, and negotiated Washington Senate Bill 5355, a sex-trafficking prevention education measure that ultimately became state law.

Success in Washington raised a harder question: could the intervention survive outside the legal and political system in which it had been created?

I went on to write and adapt more than twenty state-specific versions of the legislation, work across different legislative environments, pursue a federal pathway, and collaborate with people whose political institutions, priorities, and objections differed dramatically.

When the U.S. political environment changed after the 2024 election, several pathways narrowed or disappeared. I continued where pathways remained viable and began testing whether the underlying preventive model could travel internationally.

Eventually, practical problems became research questions.

Early work

Neuroscience and neuroethics

Scientific research first made questions about technology, intervention, and ethics concrete for me. Neuroethics pushed the question further: capability alone cannot tell us what institutions should permit, govern, or protect.

First year at Stanford

Scientific openness and research security

Conversations about research security exposed a different version of the same problem: scientific systems depend on openness, collaboration, and trust, while governments also face legitimate security concerns and geopolitical competition.

Wargaming

How institutions construct uncertain futures

Wargaming gave me a way to study uncertainty itself. Games do not simply reveal futures; their rules, temporal structures, technologies, classifications, and participants help construct what institutions come to regard as plausible.

Current work

Science, security, governance, and institutional design

My current research asks how expertise, technological change, and institutional design shape decisions about uncertain futures—from military planning and scientific collaboration to emerging technologies and public policy.

Questions

The ones I have not finished answering.

What makes an uncertain future believable?

Who gets to define plausibility, what technologies mediate that judgment, and what happens when those expectations travel into institutional planning?

How much openness can a secure scientific system sustain?

And how much security can it impose before the scientific system it is trying to protect stops functioning as an open one?

What survives translation?

When a law, model, game, technology, or educational intervention moves between institutions, what must remain fixed—and what must change for the underlying function to survive?

When should institutions act before certainty exists?

Prevention often requires acting before harm is completely visible. Governance, however, also has to distinguish foresight from overconfidence.
