About Ria Bahadur

Ria Bahadur works across science, security, law, and public institutions.

Her research and public work focus on how institutions make consequential decisions under uncertainty—from military wargaming and research security to legislation, education, and emerging technology.

The through line

Different fields have led back to a small set of recurring questions.

Her work spans sex-trafficking prevention, military wargaming, research security, science governance, education, and emerging technology. Across them, recurring questions concern who defines a problem, how institutions interpret incomplete information, and what happens when an idea moves from theory into practice.

A policy can succeed in one jurisdiction and fail in another. A wargame can make one future appear plausible while rendering another less visible. Scientific openness can enable discovery while also creating difficult questions of security, competition, and trust.

Origins

Prevention before policy.

The earliest work began with sex-trafficking prevention at twelve, focusing on how stereotypes surrounding sexual violence and trafficking could affect whether victims were recognized, believed, or reached by prevention efforts.

That work developed into educational material for students and educators, followed by a professional guide for Sexual Assault Nurse Examiners that is currently in use at WSU College of Nursing. The guide was reviewed by Dr. Victoria Patricia Sattler, who submitted it to the International Association of Forensic Nurses.

The legislative work came later: how could prevention education move from individual programs into public-school requirements?

From prevention to law

Washington proved the proposal could become law. The next twenty-plus drafts showed how much had to change for it to travel.

At seventeen, Ria Bahadur created, drafted, and negotiated Washington Senate Bill 5355. When it became law in 2023, she became the youngest sole author of enacted legislation in a U.S. state. Early implementation feedback relayed through Senator Claire Wilson in 2024–25 was positive, including an estimate that the law had already helped protect approximately 300 adolescents from trafficking-related situations. The figure is an early implementation estimate rather than a formal long-term impact evaluation, and it is expected to grow as statewide rollout continues.

She subsequently wrote and adapted more than twenty state-specific versions of the legislation, working across different legislative structures, political environments, stakeholder groups, and implementation requirements.

The effort also expanded toward a federal pathway. Following the 2024 election, several state and federal routes that had appeared viable narrowed or disappeared.

Work continued in jurisdictions where legislative openings remained, including North Dakota and Nevada, while the project increasingly moved into international settings.

From intervention to inquiry

Practical problems became research questions.

Neuroscience

Science and neuroethics

Early laboratory research in neuroscience and biotechnology was accompanied by work in neuroethics, extending scientific questions into questions of governance, intervention, and ethical limits.

UNESCO · 2024

International neurotechnology governance

At eighteen, while conducting U.S. Department of State consultations for her wargaming research, Bahadur was invited to contribute as an Academic (Medical) Stakeholder to the U.S. consultation informing UNESCO's Recommendation on the Ethics of Neurotechnology, drawing on neuroethics and work spanning optogenetics, brain organogenesis, neurogenetic disorders, and clinical neurology.

Kyoto · 2024–

Science diplomacy and the STS Forum

Invited to the Science and Technology in Society Forum Annual Meeting at nineteen, Bahadur became one of its youngest-ever invitees and attendees. She returned in 2025 and was invited again for 2026, with responsibilities expanding from rapporteurship and selected Forum convenings to formal synthesis authorship.

Stanford

Scientific openness and research security

Research-security work moved those questions from individual technologies to the institutions that produce science: universities, laboratories, governments, and international collaborations.

Wargaming

How institutions construct uncertain futures

Current research asks how scenario design, representations of technology, temporal structure, and participant expertise shape what players—and eventually institutions—come to regard as plausible futures.

Current questions

Problems that remain open.

What makes an uncertain future believable?

Who determines plausibility, what technologies mediate that judgment, and how do expectations produced in models and games travel into institutional planning?

How should scientific systems govern openness and security together?

Scientific collaboration relies on exchange and trust; security regimes often rely on restriction and risk management. Where can those logics coexist, and where do they become incompatible?

What survives institutional translation?

When a law, model, technology, game, or educational resource moves between systems, which elements must remain fixed and which must change for its underlying purpose to survive?

Earlier disciplines

The work began in more than one place.

Before her current work in science, security, law, and institutional design, Bahadur trained and competed across speech and debate, Hindustani classical music, Kathak, poetry, neuroscience, and multilingual study.

Those disciplines cultivated interests that continue to surface in her work: argument and interpretation; language and culture; performance and persuasion; scientific inquiry; and the movement of ideas between people and institutions.
