About Ria Bahadur

Work across systems, connected by a question of uncertainty.

Ria Bahadur is a researcher, legislative author, and founder whose work examines how institutions reason, adapt, and act when technology changes faster than the systems responsible for governing it.

The thread

Science, security, law, and human vulnerability are not separate problems.

Bahadur's work has developed across several environments that can look, at first glance, unrelated: sex-trafficking prevention, military wargaming, research security, science governance, education, and emerging technology.

The common problem is institutional decision-making under uncertainty. A policy can succeed in one jurisdiction and fail in another. A wargame can make one future appear plausible while rendering another invisible. Scientific openness can be both an engine of discovery and a source of security tension. Educational systems can attempt prevention while relying on assumptions that obscure the people they are intended to protect.

Across these settings, the recurring interest is less in whether a model works in theory than in what happens when it encounters people, institutions, incentives, technologies, and competing forms of expertise.

Origins

Prevention before policy.

Bahadur began working on sex-trafficking prevention at twelve. The earliest work focused on education: examining how stereotypes surrounding sexual violence and trafficking could shape whether victims were recognized, believed, or reached by prevention efforts.

That work later developed into educational material for students and educators and a professional guide for Sexual Assault Nurse Examiners.

The legislative question came afterward: if prevention education mattered, how could an intervention move beyond individual programs and become part of a durable public system?

From prevention to law

Passing one law demonstrated what legislation could do. Translating it across jurisdictions revealed what legislation could not do automatically.

At seventeen, Bahadur created, drafted, and negotiated Washington Senate Bill 5355, a sex-trafficking prevention education measure that became state law in 2023.

Washington became the first test of an intervention that had begun in education and moved into statutory design.

The next phase required writing and adapting more than twenty state-specific versions of the legislation, working across different political environments, legislative structures, stakeholder groups, and implementation constraints, while also pursuing a federal pathway.

After the 2024 election altered the political environment around several state and federal efforts, some pathways narrowed or disappeared. Work continued in jurisdictions where openings remained, while the underlying prevention model increasingly moved into international settings.

Policy across jurisdictions

The intervention stayed recognizable. Its form did not.

Moving a policy between jurisdictions is not a matter of copying statutory language. Different states allocate educational authority differently, define implementation differently, organize committees differently, and respond to different political constituencies.

The Mission 5355 legislative record therefore became a series of policy translations: preserving the preventive purpose of the intervention while changing its legal form to meet the institutional environment in which it would operate.

Successful, stalled, and abandoned versions are all part of that record. Together, they document how public policy changes when a model encounters different systems.

From intervention to inquiry

Practical problems became research questions.

Early work

Neuroscience and neuroethics

Early scientific research introduced questions of capability, intervention, and uncertainty. Neuroethics added a second problem: what becomes possible through science is not necessarily identical to what institutions should permit, govern, or protect.

Stanford

Scientific openness and research security

Research-security work shifted the problem from individual technologies to scientific systems. Open science depends on collaboration, exchange, and trust, even as states confront legitimate security concerns and geopolitical competition.

Wargaming

How institutions construct uncertain futures

Wargaming provided a way to study uncertainty directly. Scenario design, representations of technology, temporal structure, and participant expertise do not simply describe possible futures; they help determine which futures institutions come to regard as plausible.

Current research

Science, security, and institutional design

Current work examines how expertise, technological change, institutional design, and competing forms of authority shape decisions about uncertain futures—from military planning and research security to scientific collaboration and emerging technology governance.

Current questions

Problems that remain open.

What makes an uncertain future believable?

Who determines plausibility, what technologies mediate that judgment, and how do those expectations travel from models and games into institutional planning?

How should scientific systems govern openness and security together?

Scientific collaboration relies on exchange and trust; national security systems are often organized around restriction and risk. Where can those logics coexist, and where do they become incompatible?

What survives institutional translation?

When a law, model, technology, game, or educational intervention moves between systems, which elements must remain fixed and which must change for the underlying function to survive?

When should institutions act before certainty exists?

Prevention and strategic foresight often require action before harm or opportunity is fully visible. Governance must therefore distinguish responsible anticipation from institutional overconfidence.

Beyond current work

Earlier interests remain part of the record.

Bahadur's earlier work includes speech and debate, Hindustani classical music, Kathak, poetry, neuroscience, community advocacy, and the study of multiple languages.

These are preserved as part of the site's archive rather than presented as parallel professional identities. They provide context for the interests in communication, culture, argument, systems, and interdisciplinary work that followed.
