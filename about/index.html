About Ria Bahadur

Neural stem cells. State law. Wargames. Science policy.

The route is not linear. The recurring instinct is to identify the point at which action is still possible — before a downstream failure becomes the only thing an institution can see.

Early teens

Research begins in neuroscience.

Training in neural stem cells, Cas9 gene editing, brain organogenesis, Piezo1 mechanobiology, immunostaining, image analysis, and patch-clamp electrophysiology at the Pathak Laboratory.

Age 17

Washington SB 5355 becomes law.

Bahadur created, drafted, and negotiated the trafficking-prevention education measure that passed the Washington Senate 48–0 and House 97–0.

Age 18

Science governance moves into the picture.

Participation in a National Academies research-security roundtable at Hoover and stakeholder input, by U.S. Department of State invitation, on UNESCO's Recommendation on the Ethics of Neurotechnology.

Age 19

Kyoto.

Invited to the STS Forum Annual Meeting as one of its youngest-ever invitees and attendees; selected for rapporteur responsibilities.

2025–26

Research, institutions, and building converge.

Hoover Project Innovation, Stanford STS Honors, NATO CCDCOE, Mission 5355, a stealth venture, a patent-pending independent invention, and the 2026 FUSE Fellowship.

The throughline

Preemption is not just early action. It is knowing what “early” means.

Science and human safety accumulate over time, while institutions often govern through snapshots. Bahadur's work asks where signals first become legible, how uncertainty is constructed, and how systems can act before the window closes.
