Research Security & Scientific Governance

How should open scientific systems operate under strategic competition?

Research on scientific openness, technological leadership, international collaboration, security, governance, expertise, and public trust.

The problem

Science depends on exchange. Security can require limits on exchange.

Modern scientific systems depend on the movement of people, information, methods, capital, equipment, and ideas across institutions and national borders.

The same networks can also carry strategically consequential knowledge and technological capability. Research-security policy therefore operates inside a genuine institutional tension: protecting scientific and national interests without undermining the openness, collaboration, and trust on which scientific progress depends.

Bahadur's current work examines how universities, national laboratories, governments, industry, and international scientific communities navigate that boundary—and how decisions about cooperation, protection, priority, and trust are made.

Structural questions

The research separates four problems that are often treated as one.

PRIORITY

Where must the United States lead?

Which scientific and technological domains are strategically consequential enough to justify sustained national capability, investment, or protection?

COOPERATION

Where is collaboration still necessary?

Which scientific problems remain structurally international even when governments face greater pressure to restrict research relationships?

GOVERNANCE

How does expertise reach decisions?

How should scientific knowledge move through agencies, laboratories, universities, advisory bodies, and political institutions without losing either technical integrity or democratic legitimacy?

TRUST

What makes scientific institutions credible?

How do institutions earn and maintain trust among scientists, policymakers, partners, and the public when scientific claims are increasingly contested or mediated by new technologies?

Independent research

Research Security in the Changing Global Innovation Environment

Alongside Project Innovation, Bahadur is developing an independent study of research security across the contemporary scientific ecosystem.

The work examines how research-security regimes interact with scientific practice: international collaboration, institutional incentives, technological competition, researcher mobility, university governance, public trust, and the different ways scientific communities understand risk.

SECURITY

Protection without indiscriminate restriction

Examination of how institutions distinguish strategically meaningful risks from policies that impose broad costs on legitimate scientific exchange.

PRACTICE

Policy as experienced by scientists

Analysis of the distance that can emerge between formal security regimes and the incentives, norms, workflows, and epistemic practices of scientific communities.

SYSTEM

Research security as institutional design

A broader inquiry into which organizations should make security judgments, how responsibilities should be distributed, and how policy can adapt as scientific and geopolitical conditions change.

Hoover Project Innovation

Current research brings these questions into a sustained study of scientific innovation, technological leadership, international cooperation, research security, governance, and public trust.

Analytical frame

Security policy and scientific practice do not always describe risk in the same way.

Security regimes

Governments often organize research risk through categories such as strategic technology, foreign influence, intellectual property, critical capability, access, and adversarial intent.

Those categories determine what receives scrutiny, what is restricted, and which relationships become institutionally costly.

Scientific practice

Research communities organize knowledge differently: through disciplinary norms, laboratories, tacit expertise, publication, collaboration, reputation, mobility, and informal networks of trust.

Research-security policy becomes consequential where these two ways of organizing scientific activity meet.

International cooperation

Competition does not eliminate interdependence.

Scientific competition increasingly shapes national policy, but many research problems remain impossible—or substantially harder—to solve within national boundaries alone.

The research therefore treats cooperation and competition as overlapping conditions rather than mutually exclusive choices. Different technologies, disciplines, institutions, and stages of research may require different combinations of openness, partnership, monitoring, and protection.

A central problem is institutional precision: determining where restrictions address genuine vulnerabilities and where they instead weaken the scientific capability they are intended to protect.

Trust & scientific authority

Security is not the only condition under which scientific institutions operate.

Scientific institutions also depend on credibility: among researchers, policymakers, international partners, funders, industry, and the public.

That has made trust an increasingly important part of Bahadur's science-governance work, including questions of expertise, institutional reputation, AI-mediated information, validation, scientific communication, and the ways claims acquire authority.

The 2026 IBM & NAA–STS Forum High-Level Workshop extended this work into questions of AI, scientific influence, misinformation, validation, and public trust.
