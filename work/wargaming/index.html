Wargaming & Strategic Foresight

How military wargames turn uncertain futures into judgments.

Research and game design examining how time, technology, expertise, scenarios, and adjudication shape what players—and eventually institutions—come to regard as plausible.

The question

A wargame does not simply represent a future. Its design helps determine which futures can be seen.

Military wargames are often used to explore conflict before it occurs: testing plans, technologies, assumptions, adversarial responses, and strategic choices under conditions of uncertainty.

Bahadur's research focuses on the structure of that process. How does changing the pace of a game alter what participants notice? What happens when technological capabilities are represented as fixed, delayed, evolving, or uncertain? Who has authority to decide whether an action succeeds? And how does the expertise of the people in the room affect which futures appear credible?

These are not only questions about gameplay. Wargame judgments can inform planning, doctrine, procurement, research priorities, and later exercises. The project therefore studies both what happens inside a game and how its conclusions acquire meaning outside it.

Stanford STS Honors

Credibility by Design

Credibility by Design: How Military Wargames Shape Plausibility Judgments investigates how scenario framing, temporal pacing, capability sequencing, technological mediation, adjudication authority, and expertise affect judgments in professional military wargames.

The thesis asks not only which outcomes a game produces, but how participants explain why a particular future is credible—and how those explanations travel into institutional settings beyond the exercise.

RESEARCH DESIGN

Mixed methods

Archival-historical analysis, comparative case study, controlled design variation, structured observation, and pre- and post-exercise interviews.

DESIGN VARIABLES

Time, technology & authority

The research varies and compares temporal pacing, capability sequencing, technological representation, scenario framing, participant expertise, and adjudication structure.

ANALYSIS

Credibility language

Particular attention is paid to how participants justify plausibility: what evidence, assumptions, expertise, models, and institutional norms they invoke when defending a future as credible.

INSTITUTIONAL EFFECT

Beyond the game

The project examines how judgments generated through games can enter subsequent planning, doctrine, procurement, research, and future exercise design.

Selected presentation · 2026

From Kriegsspiel to SIGNAL

From Kriegsspiel to SIGNAL: The Sociotechnical Evolution of Digital Wargaming and Its Implications for Strategic Scalability traces the changing technological infrastructure of professional military gaming.

The project examines what changes when a practice historically grounded in maps, tables, human adjudication, and bounded scenarios incorporates digital simulation, cyber realism, computational systems, and increasingly AI-mediated processes.

Game design · 2025–2026

Wargaming at the Forum for American-Chinese Exchange.

Designing the exercise

As Wargaming Director for Stanford FACES, Bahadur designed and led exercises for American and Chinese student delegates addressing questions including lethal autonomous weapons systems, geopolitical escalation, and nuclear deterrence.

The exercises required converting broad strategic problems into playable systems: defining actors, information, decision sequences, capabilities, constraints, uncertainty, and adjudication.

Learning from iteration

Successive 2025 and 2026 designs provided a practical setting for examining how small changes in structure alter participant behavior and interpretation.

This design experience helped motivate the thesis's controlled variation approach: treating features of a game as variables that can themselves be studied rather than accepted as neutral containers for strategic reasoning.

What the research compares

The game is not a neutral container.

TIME

Temporal structure

Turn length, compression, sequencing, deadlines, and the pace at which technological or strategic change can occur.

TECHNOLOGY

Capability representation

Which technologies exist, how mature they appear, what uncertainty surrounds them, and whether they can evolve during play.

EXPERTISE

Who gets to know

The distribution of technical, military, policy, and other forms of expertise—and whose judgment receives authority.

SCENARIO

Problem framing

The assumptions, actors, starting conditions, boundaries, and strategic questions built into the exercise.

ADJUDICATION

What counts as possible

Human judgment, rules, models, data, simulation, and computational systems used to resolve uncertain actions and outcomes.

LEGITIMACY

Why a future is believed

The language participants use to defend an outcome as realistic, credible, likely, strategically relevant, or institutionally actionable.
